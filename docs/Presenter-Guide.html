The three golden rules

1It is practice money

Nothing here is real. You cannot win or lose actual rupees. Say "practice money" or "virtual money", never "invest your money".

2It describes, it never advises

The site shows what the charts are saying. It never says "buy this". If a visitor asks "should I buy?", the answer is: "That is not what it is for. It shows what the signals read; the decision is yours, and a real decision needs a real adviser."

3Be honest about the limits

In our own tests the strategies were right about 50 times out of 100, which is the same as flipping a coin. We show that openly on purpose. It makes the site trustworthy.

Say this, not that

Instead ofSay

Companies, shares and the stock market

Start with these five ideas. Every later page leans on them.

1A company, and a share (also called a stock)

In simple words

Imagine a pizza shop. The shop is cut into a million tiny slices, and anyone can own a slice. A share is one slice of a company. If you own a slice, you own a tiny piece of the shop, and if the shop does well, your slice becomes more valuable.

In finance words

A share (or stock) is a unit of ownership in a company. Companies sell shares to raise money to grow. A shareholder owns part of the company and may get a share of the profit, called a dividend. A company whose shares anyone can buy is called listed.

On the screen

You search for a company by name (a small spelling slip is fine) and its price chart appears. You can buy and sell shares of it with practice money.

2Why does a share price go up and down?

In simple words

Think of a popular toy at school. If everyone wants it and few have it, the price goes up. If nobody wants it, the price falls. A share price is simply what buyers and sellers agree on right now.

In finance words

Price is set by supply and demand in the market. More buyers than sellers pushes the price up. More sellers than buyers pushes it down. News, company profits, interest rates and the general mood of investors (sentiment) all change how many people want to buy or sell.

On the screen

The big number at the top of the chart is the latest price. Next to it is the change since yesterday's close, in rupees and in percent (green for up, red for down).

3The stock exchange: NSE and BSE

In simple words

An exchange is like a big, well-organised market (a mela with strict rules) where all buying and selling of shares happens in one place, fairly and openly.

In finance words

A stock exchange is a regulated marketplace where listed shares are traded. India has two main ones: the NSE (National Stock Exchange) and the BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange). Many big companies are listed on both. The regulator that watches over them is SEBI.

On the screen

You can search both listings. A company's short code, called a ticker symbol (like RELIANCE or TCS), is shown next to its name. It is the company's nickname on the exchange.

4An index: Nifty 50 and Sensex

In simple words

An index is a class scoreboard. Instead of looking at 50 students one by one, you look at their average marks. The Nifty 50 is the average of the 50 biggest companies on the NSE, so it tells you how 'the market' is doing overall.

In finance words

An index tracks a basket of shares. The Nifty 50 covers 50 large NSE companies, weighted by size. The Sensex covers 30 large BSE companies. They are used as the benchmark: to say whether a company did better or worse than the market.

On the screen

The 'Relative strength' strategy compares a company with the Nifty 50 (or Sensex for a BSE listing).

5Market hours, and the 'last close'

In simple words

The market is like a shop with opening hours. Outside them nothing is bought or sold, so the price stays where it stopped.

In finance words

The Indian market trades 9:15 am to 3:30 pm, Monday to Friday, except holidays. The last close is the final price of the previous session. Outside hours the screen shows it, with a note that the market is closed.

On the screen

The line under the price says 'Market closed (opens Monday at 9:15 am), last close' or tells you the market is open. While it is open, parts of the screen refresh themselves every few seconds. Prices come from Yahoo Finance and can be a few minutes late.

Reading a price: volume, candles, trends and risk

Now the language of the chart. Every indicator later is built from these.

1Volume

In simple words

Volume is how many shoppers walked into the mall today. A big crowd means lots of interest. A price move that many people joined is more believable than one a few people made.

In finance words

Volume is the number of shares traded in a period. High volume on a move confirms it. Low volume suggests weak conviction. Volume is shown separately from price because it measures participation, not value.

On the screen

The bars under the chart. Green bars are days the price rose, red bars are days it fell.

2Open, High, Low, Close and the candle

In simple words

Each day has a little story: where the price started, the highest and lowest it reached, and where it finished. A candle draws that story as one small shape. Green means the day ended higher than it began. Red means lower.

In finance words

The four daily prices are OHLC: open, high, low, close. A candlestick shows them: the thick body runs from open to close and the thin wicks reach the high and low. A green body means close above open.

Left: a green candle (the price rose). Right: a red candle (the price fell).

On the screen

Every bar on the main chart is one candle: a day, a week or a month, depending on the Day, Week and Month buttons.

3Trend: up, down or sideways

In simple words

A trend is the direction a price has been walking. Up like climbing stairs, down like descending them, or sideways like walking along a corridor.

In finance words

A trend is the general direction of price over time: an uptrend (rising highs and lows), a downtrend, or a sideways (range-bound) market. Trend-following aims to go with the trend, not against it.

On the screen

Most strategies and indicators are really different ways of asking: is there a trend, and how strong is it?

4Risk and return

In simple words

Return is what you might gain. Risk is how bumpy the road is on the way, and how much you could lose. A very bumpy road can lead somewhere great, or nowhere. Bigger possible gains come with bigger possible losses.

In finance words

Return is the percentage gain or loss on money invested. Risk is the uncertainty of that return, often measured by volatility (how much the price swings). Higher expected return generally needs accepting higher risk. There is no free lunch.

5Diversification

In simple words

Do not carry all your eggs in one basket. If you drop it, they all break. Putting eggs in different baskets means one accident does not ruin everything.

In finance words

Diversification means spreading money across different companies, sectors and asset types, so one bad result hurts less. It lowers the risk specific to one company, but it does not remove the risk of the whole market falling.

On the screen

The ring on the left shows how your practice money is spread across stocks, ETFs, bonds, futures, options and cash.

Your portfolio, practice trading, ETFs and bonds

What you hold, how the site lets you practise, and two things to hold besides shares.

1Portfolio, cash, profit and return

In simple words

Your portfolio is your bag of things you own, plus the cash you have not spent. If you start with Rs 1,00,000 and your bag is worth Rs 1,05,000 a week later, you are up Rs 5,000. That is a 5% return.

In finance words

A portfolio is the collection of all your holdings plus cash. Its value is cash plus the current market value of everything held. Profit or loss (P&L) is value minus what you put in, and the return is that as a percentage. If you buy the same share at different prices, your average buy price is the total paid divided by the units.

On the screen

'Portfolio value' at the top shows the total and the percent change since you started. 'Bought: what you hold' lists each holding with its profit or loss. 'Sold: recent sales' lists your last six sales.

2Paper trading

In simple words

Cricketers practise in the nets before a real match. Paper trading is the nets for the stock market: real prices, pretend money, nothing to lose, plenty to learn.

In finance words

Paper trading (also called virtual or simulated trading) means placing trades with pretend money at real market prices, to learn how buying, selling, profit and loss work without financial risk. It is a standard way to practise a strategy before committing real capital.

On the screen

You pick your starting practice money (Rs 50,000, 1 lakh, 5 lakh, 10 lakh or your own amount). The Buy, Sell and Exit all buttons below the chart place practice trades. No brokerage or tax is charged here; real trading has both.

3ETF (exchange-traded fund)

In simple words

An ETF is a lunchbox with a bit of everything. Instead of buying one food item, you buy one box that has many items inside. One ETF can hold a whole basket of companies, or gold, or silver.

In finance words

An ETF is a fund that holds a basket of assets (shares of an index, gold, silver or bonds) and is bought and sold on the exchange like a share. A Nifty 50 ETF simply copies the Nifty 50. ETFs give instant diversification with one purchase and usually low costs.

On the screen

In the Buy pop-up, the 'ETFs and bonds' tab lists Nifty 50, Bank, IT, Gold and Silver ETFs, among others.

4Bond

In simple words

A bond is lending money. You lend to the government or a company, and they promise to give it back on a set date, plus a little extra (interest) for using it. It is like lending your friend Rs 100 and getting back Rs 106 next year.

In finance words

A bond is a loan to an issuer (a government or company) that pays regular interest (the coupon) and returns the principal at maturity. Bond prices move opposite to interest rates. Government bonds are generally lower-risk than company bonds, and bonds are usually calmer than shares.

On the screen

The same 'ETFs and bonds' tab includes bond ETFs such as the Bharat Bond ETF and government-security (G-Sec) ETFs, plus 'liquid' ETFs that behave like cash.

Cannot sell what you do not own. In the practice account you can only sell shares you hold, and you can only buy what your cash allows. If cash runs short, the Buy button says so.

Futures and options

These are called derivatives: their value comes from (is derived from) something else, such as a share. They are riskier, so go slowly.

Future

In simple words

A farmer and a shopkeeper agree today that the shopkeeper will buy 100 kg of mangoes next month at Rs 80 a kilo, whatever the price turns out to be. Both are bound to the deal. If the price rises to Rs 100, the shopkeeper gained. If it falls to Rs 60, the farmer gained.

In finance words

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price on a fixed date. It is an obligation for both sides. You put down only a small deposit, the margin, so gains and losses are large compared with what you put in: this is leverage.

Long and short

In simple words

Long = you gain if the price goes up. Short = you gain if it goes down, like betting a ticket price will fall.

In finance words

Going long profits from a rise; going short profits from a fall. In this app you can go long or short in futures. Shares can only be bought, then sold.

Margin, lot and expiry

In simple words

Margin is a deposit, like the one you pay to rent a bike. A lot is a fixed pack size, like eggs sold by the dozen. Expiry is the date the deal ends, like a milk packet's date.

In finance words

Margin: a deposit that backs the position (here 15% of the contract's value). A lot: the fixed quantity in one contract. Expiry: the contract's end date (here the last Tuesday of the month). If losses use up the margin, the position is closed automatically.

Option: call and put

In simple words

You pay a small fee to reserve a phone at today's price for a week. If the price jumps, you use your reservation and save money. If it drops, you just walk away and lose only the small fee. An option is a right, not a must.

A call = the right to buy (you hope the price goes up). A put = the right to sell (you hope it goes down).

In finance words

An option gives the buyer the right but not the obligation to buy (a call) or sell (a put) an asset at a fixed price, the strike price, on or before expiry. The price paid for that right is the premium. The most the buyer can lose is the premium, but it can lose all of its value quickly, especially near expiry.

The chart and its tools

What the lines, bands and bars on the chart mean. You met candles and volume on .

A chart with its tools

A made-up example to show the parts. A real chart has candles instead of one line.

Moving average (50-day and 200-day)

In simple words

A day's price jumps about. The average of the last 50 days smooths out the bumps, like finding your average marks over a term instead of one test. The 50-day line shows the recent mood and the 200-day line the long-term mood.

In finance words

A simple moving average (SMA) is the mean of the last N closes. It filters noise and shows direction. Price above both lines, with the 50-day above the 200-day, is a classic uptrend. A golden cross is the 50-day rising above the 200-day; a death cross is the reverse.

On the screen

The orange line is the 50-day average and the navy line the 200-day.

The grey band (Bollinger Bands)

In simple words

It is the price's usual lane, like the lane markings on a road. Most days the price stays inside it. When the price swings wildly the lane gets wider; when it is calm the lane narrows.

In finance words

Bollinger Bands (John Bollinger) are a 20-day average with bands 2 standard deviations above and below. They widen with volatility. A close outside the band is unusual for that stock, but in a strong trend it can stay there.

On the screen

The grey shaded area around the price line.

Fibonacci levels

In simple words

Drop a ball down a staircase. It does not roll straight down: it pauses on certain steps. Fibonacci lines mark the steps where a price often pauses after a big rise or fall.

In finance words

After a swing, prices often retrace part of the move before continuing. Fibonacci retracements mark 23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8% and 78.6% of the swing; extensions (127.2%, 161.8%) mark possible targets beyond it. They are a guide for support and resistance, not a prediction.

On the screen

Switch on Fibonacci in the chart controls. The lines are drawn from the highest and lowest price in the period you are viewing.

Fibonacci levels, drawn

Support and resistance

In simple words

Support is a floor the price bounced off before. Resistance is a ceiling it could not get through. They are like the floor and ceiling of a room.

In finance words

Support is a price zone where buying has repeatedly stopped declines; resistance is a zone where selling has stopped advances. A break through either is often read as a change in the balance of buyers and sellers.

1, 2 or 4 charts; Day, Week or Month

In simple words

Like zooming a map: one chart for the big picture, or several side by side to compare views. Day, Week and Month decide whether each candle is one day, one week or one month.

In finance words

Looking at multiple timeframes is standard analysis: the higher timeframe shows the main trend, the lower one the timing. The 1, 2 and 4 buttons set how many charts you see, each in a timeframe of your choice.

Indicators, and how to read a gauge

An indicator is a number worked out from price (and sometimes volume) that answers one small question about a chart. The six gauges on the right of the screen each answer a different question.

How to read any gauge on the screen

Every gauge is a coloured bar with a black pointer.

  • Red on the left: this indicator leans down.
  • Green on the right: it leans up.
  • Middle: neutral.

The badge at the top right says Leans up, Leans down or Neutral, and the word under the bar says what the reading is, such as 'Balanced' or 'Stretched high'.

Some indicators measure a stretch (RSI, Z-score, the band). For these the bar is turned round: 'stretched high' sits at the red end, because a price that ran up too fast often cools off.

One idea to repeat to visitors. An indicator is a clue, not an answer. Six clues can disagree, and that is normal. Never treat one gauge as a promise.

1RSI (speed)

In simple words

RSI is the speedometer of a price. It runs from 0 to 100. Near 100 the price has been racing up very fast and may need a rest. Near 0 it has been falling fast and may bounce. But like a fast car, it can keep going.

In finance words

Relative Strength Index (J. Welles Wilder): RSI = 100 - 100 / (1 + average gain / average loss), using Wilder's smoothing over 14 days. Above 70 is conventionally overbought, below 30 oversold. In strong trends it can stay at an extreme for a long time, so it is a hint, not a signal on its own.

On the screen

'RSI (speed)'. 'Overbought (70)' is the red left end, 'Oversold (30)' the green right end. The word under it says Balanced, Overbought or Oversold.

2MACD (momentum)

In simple words

Two friends run along a track, one quick and one steady. When the quick one pulls ahead, the pace is picking up. When the steady one catches up, the pace is fading. MACD compares a quick and a slow average of the price in the same way.

In finance words

Moving Average Convergence Divergence (Gerald Appel): the MACD line is the 12-day exponential average minus the 26-day one. The signal line is the 9-day average of the MACD line, and the histogram is the gap between them. MACD above its signal line means rising momentum, below means fading.

On the screen

'MACD (momentum)'. The words are Rising, Slightly rising, Slightly falling or Falling, with the pointer to the right when momentum is rising.

3Trend strength (ADX)

In simple words

ADX is the wind speed, not the wind direction. A weak wind means the price is just drifting about. A strong wind means the price is really heading somewhere. Two helper lines say whether the buyers or the sellers are pushing.

In finance words

Average Directional Index (J. Welles Wilder): a 14-day smoothed measure of trend strength, either way. Below about 20 the market is range-bound; above 20 it is trending. Direction comes from +DI (upward pressure) against -DI (downward pressure).

On the screen

'Trend strength (ADX)'. The pointer shows who is in the lead: right with 'Buyers lead', left with 'Sellers lead'.

Indicators, continued

Three more gauges, and a word on how they fit together.

1Z-score

In simple words

A Z-score is how far today's price is from its usual price, like your height compared with your class. Close to the class average means normal. Far taller or far shorter than everyone means unusual. A price very far above its usual level often drifts back down.

In finance words

z = (price - 20-day average) / standard deviation over the same 20 days. It measures the price in units of standard deviations. Beyond +2 or -2 is unusual (about 1 day in 20 if prices were bell-shaped), which mean-reversion traders watch. In strong trends extremes can persist.

On the screen

'Z-score'. The words are Stretched high (above +2), Normal, or Stretched low (below -2). It is turned round: stretched high is the red end.

2On-Balance Volume (OBV)

In simple words

Count the shoppers who come into a shop. On days the price goes up, add the crowd to a score. On days it goes down, take it away. If the score keeps climbing, the crowd turns up more on the up days. It tells you whether the crowd is busier when the price rises or when it falls.

In finance words

On-Balance Volume (Joseph Granville, 1963): a running total that adds a day's volume on an up-close and subtracts it on a down-close. A rising OBV suggests accumulation (buyers in control); a falling OBV suggests distribution. If price makes a new high but OBV does not, that divergence is a warning. The app uses the last 20 days' net flow, from -100% to +100%.

On the screen

'On-Balance Volume'. The words are 'Buyers backing it' (pointer right), 'Sellers backing it' (pointer left) or 'Balanced'. It is the one gauge that looks at volume, not just price.

3Bollinger band (where the price sits)

In simple words

On the grey band was the price's usual lane. This gauge tells you where in the lane the price is right now: near the top edge, near the bottom edge or in the middle.

In finance words

%B = (price - lower band) / (upper band - lower band). Near the top of the band the price is stretched high; near the bottom it is stretched low. Outside the band is an extreme. Band width shows volatility.

On the screen

'Bollinger band'. 'Top of range' is the red left end, 'Bottom of range' the green right end. The words say Stretched high, Inside range or Stretched low.

Why do the six gauges often disagree?

They answer different questions. RSI asks 'how fast?', MACD 'is the pace picking up?', ADX 'is there a real trend?', the Z-score and the band ask 'how stretched?', and OBV asks 'is the crowd behind it?'.

It is like asking six different doctors six different questions. Their answers do not have to match. The skill is in seeing the whole picture.

In finance words: the indicators fall into families: momentum (RSI, MACD), trend strength (ADX), volatility and stretch (Z-score, Bollinger), and volume (OBV). Using one from each family is better than using several from the same family, because they add different information.

All six are lagging: they are calculated from past prices, so they describe, they do not foresee.

Hover for help

Next to every indicator name is a small i. Hover over it (or the badge) for a one-paragraph description. The round chat button in the corner can also answer 'what is the Z-score?' in simple or adult language.

Strategies, votes and the dial

Indicators are clues. A strategy is a set of rules that turns clues into a call. Five of them come from the CMT (Chartered Market Technician) curriculum, the professional exam in chart analysis.

The vote

Each strategy looks at the chart and gives one of three answers, called a vote:

  • ▲ Up: its rules say the reading leans up.
  • ▼ Down: leans down.
  • – Neutral: no clear reading. 'n/a' means not enough history to judge.

A vote describes what the rules see today. It is not a promise about tomorrow.

The dial (combined strategy reading)

The dial adds up the five votes (+1 for each Up, -1 for each Down).

It says Strongly up, Leans up, Balanced, Leans down or Strongly down, and below it: '3 of 5 strategies read down, 1 up, 1 neutral'. The ML model is not counted.

Think of five friends voting on whether to take an umbrella. Each looks at the sky in a different way. The dial just counts their votes. It does not know whether it will rain.

1Fusion stage (the Winner's Circle trend test)

In simple words

It asks one question: is this company in a clear, steady climb and doing better than the market? Imagine a runner who is both running uphill and overtaking the rest of the class. The strategy likes such runners. If instead the runner is sliding downhill, it votes Down.

In finance words

From CMT Level III, Chapter 8 (fusion analysis). A company is in a clear uptrend when the price is above a rising 200-day average and the 50-day is above the 200-day, and it also outperforms the Nifty over six months (the trend and momentum test). That votes Up. A clear downtrend votes Down. Everything else is Neutral. The four trend stages are: clear uptrend, base of an uptrend, base of a downtrend, clear downtrend.

On the screen

First row of the table. Full view shows a scrollable guide to the four groups, with plain names: Winner's Circle (trend, quality and valuation all pass), Strong contender (trend plus one of the two), Momentum only (trend, but not quality or valuation) and Watchlist (the trend has not confirmed).

2Dow Theory

In simple words

Climbing stairs: each step is higher than the last, and each rest stop is higher too. That is an uptrend. If the peaks and the dips both keep getting lower, it is like walking down the stairs: a downtrend.

In finance words

From CMT Level I (Charles Dow). An uptrend is a sequence of higher highs and higher lows; a downtrend is lower highs and lower lows. The trend is confirmed only when both agree. The app marks a swing point once five bars on each side confirm it, so it never looks ahead.

On the screen

Second row. Votes Up when the last two swing highs and swing lows are both rising, Down when both are falling, Neutral when they disagree.

1Relative strength (against the market)

In simple words

A race. If the whole class improves by 5 marks and you improve by 10, you did better than the class even though everyone improved. This strategy checks whether a company is running faster than the Nifty 50. Leaders tend to stay leaders for a while.

In finance words

A CMT core topic. It divides the stock's price by the index to get a relative strength line. It votes Up when that line is above its own 50-day average and has risen over three months, Down when it is below and has fallen, else Neutral. (Do not confuse it with the RSI indicator, which is different.)

On the screen

Third row. The 'What it sees' column says, for example, '-1.9% vs the market (3 months)'.

2Wyckoff: effort versus result

In simple words

Push a heavy door. Big effort and the door swings open: a real move. Big effort and it barely moves: someone on the other side is pushing back. In markets, effort is volume and result is how far the price moved.

In finance words

From CMT Level II (Richard Wyckoff). Effort = volume at least 1.4 times its 20-day average. Result = the price move compared with a typical day's range (ATR). Big effort with a big result follows the move (Up or Down). Big effort with a small result is a stall: near the low of the recent range that is absorption (Up), near the high it is distribution (Down).

On the screen

Fourth row. 'What it sees' shows, for example, 'Volume 1.8x usual'.

3Fibonacci confluence

In simple words

You saw the staircase on . This strategy checks whether the price is resting on one of the steps right now. A price that dipped after a climb and is pausing on a step may be getting ready to climb again.

In finance words

From CMT Levels I and II. Over the last six months' swing it checks whether the price sits within 4% of the swing's size from the 38.2%, 50% or 61.8% retracement. A pullback in an uptrend that holds there votes Up (support); a bounce in a downtrend that stalls there votes Down (resistance). 'Confluence' means several clues meeting at one level.

On the screen

Fifth row. 'Not at a key level' means Neutral.

4The ML model (machine learning)

In simple words

We showed a computer thousands of days of old prices from 137 companies and let it learn which clues usually came before a rise or a fall, a bit like learning which clouds usually bring rain. It then says something like '52% chance of a higher close tomorrow'. But day-to-day moves are mostly luck, so it is only about as good as a coin toss.

In finance words

A logistic regression (a simple, explainable model, not deep learning) with 18 inputs: the five strategy votes plus RSI, Stochastic RSI, Bollinger %B, Z-score, MACD, ADX lean, On-Balance Volume, recent returns, volume, volatility and distance from the 200-day average. It was trained on the first 70% of the dates and tested on the last 30%, data it had never seen. Out of sample it scored about 50 to 51%, the same as always guessing the more common direction. It shows a lean only at 54% or more (Up) or 46% or less (Down).

On the screen

The sixth row, marked 'ML model'. Full view shows a bar chart of which inputs pushed today's number up or down. It is a second opinion, not counted in the dial.

Key message for visitors. The strategies and the model are tools for understanding a chart, from a real professional curriculum. We tested them honestly and they were about as good as a coin toss at predicting. We show that on purpose. That honesty is the point of the project.

The back-test, and why honesty matters

The last column of the strategies table is the one visitors ask about most.

What is a back-test?

In simple words

Before an exam you practise with last year's question paper to see how well your method works. A back-test does the same for a strategy: we replay old prices and see how often the strategy would have been right. Then we check it again on a part it had never seen.

In finance words

A back-test applies a rule set to historical data to measure how it would have performed. A fair back-test avoids look-ahead bias (using information that was not available at the time) and overfitting (tuning rules until they fit the past). Here the rules were fixed first and the results are reported on the dates held back.

How to read 'Back-test on this company'

In simple words

For the company on screen, the column says how often that strategy's Up or Down vote came true. '49% next day' means: on the days it said Up or Down for this company, the price moved that way the very next day 49 times out of 100. '41% over 20 days' means the same, about a month later.

In finance words

Each row's figure is that company's own hit rate: the share of Up or Down votes followed by a move in that direction over the next day and the next 20 trading days, measured on that company's own history (roughly 900 to 1,100 calls for Fusion stage, Dow Theory and Relative strength, around 300 for Fibonacci and only about 150 for Wyckoff, which votes on far fewer days). Hover over a number to see how many past calls it rests on and the all-company average for comparison. If a strategy made fewer than 30 calls, the row says 'too few past calls to judge'. For the ML model the figure is from days it was not trained on.

On the screen

The header reads 'Back-test on this company: how often it matched the next move'. A company with no saved history shows the all-company average and the header says so.

The honest headline

The next-day numbers are almost all (about 9 in 10) between 45% and 55%. The 20-day numbers spread wider, but their typical value is still close to 50%. A coin toss is 50%.

Do not read a 53% on one company and a 48% on another as 'one is better'. Per-company numbers wobble by a few points by luck alone.

Why show numbers that are not impressive?

Because it is true. Share prices from one day to the next are very hard to forecast. If a website claimed 80% accuracy you should be suspicious.

This site's promise is different: it teaches how professionals read a chart, and it is honest about how far reading goes. That is why the screen says the strategies describe, they do not recommend.

So what are the strategies good for?

In simple words

A weather forecast cannot tell you if it will rain on your cricket match, but knowing how to read the clouds still helps you decide whether to carry a raincoat. The strategies help you understand what the chart is doing and why, and to ask better questions.

In finance words

In practice technical signals are used as inputs to a process that also includes risk management (position sizing, stop-losses) and the investor's time horizon, not as stand-alone predictors. The value is in discipline and understanding, not in a guaranteed edge. Past results do not guarantee future results.

On the screen

Click Full view on any row. A pop-up shows a chart of what that strategy is looking at, a key, and a plain explanation of what it is doing today for this company.

Using the site, and a 3-minute demo

How a visitor uses it, step by step

  1. Start. Choose a name (a first name or nickname) and how much practice money. Returning visitors choose 'Returning user' and type the same name. Portfolios have no passwords, so anyone who knows a name can open it.
  2. Search. Type a company, for example Reliance, and press Enter.
  3. Look. Read the chart. Try Week, 5Y and Fibonacci.
  4. Read. Look at the six gauges, the dial and the strategies table. Click one Full view.
  5. Practise. Press Buy, choose how much, buy. The pop-up stays open for the next order.
  6. Watch. Your holdings, ring and rank update. Check the leaderboard.
  7. Ask. Use the round chat button for any word you do not know.

  • This guide: the small blue document button above the chat button opens this guide in a new tab.

  • Dashboard view: off gives the simple phone-style layout.
  • Leaderboard: ranked by percent return since the start, so small portfolios can win.
  • Slow or odd? The site is on free hosting and may take a moment to wake. Prices may be a few minutes behind.
  • An error message? Stay calm. A grey note appears with a reference number to quote to the admin. It is not your fault.

Questions you will be asked

Is this real money?
No. Everything is virtual. Prices are real, the money is pretend.
Can I make money with this?
It is for learning, not earning. It teaches how to read charts. Real trading carries real risk and needs a real adviser.
Should I buy this share?
The site does not tell you that. It shows what the signals read. The decision, and the responsibility, is yours.
Is the prediction accurate?
There is no prediction. The strategies describe the chart, and in our tests they matched the next move about 50% of the time, like a coin toss.
Why does it say 'leans' instead of 'will'?
Because nobody can know the future. 'Leans' means the clues point that way, not that it will happen.
Are the prices live?
They come from Yahoo Finance and can be a few minutes late. When the market is closed it shows the last close.
Why are the futures and options prices 'calculated'?
Real exchange prices for them are not freely available, so the app works them out from the share price with standard formulas. They are learning estimates.
What is the CMT?
The Chartered Market Technician exam, a professional qualification in chart analysis. The strategies here come from its curriculum.
What does the ML model do?
It learned from old prices which clues came before ups and downs, and gives a chance. In tests it was about as good as a coin toss, so it is a second opinion.
Who made it?
Freya Shah, from Mayo College Girls School, using what she has learned, with an AI coding assistant to write the code.
Why is there no password?
Names are not private. Use a first name or nickname and no personal details.
Which indicator is the best?
None on its own. They answer different questions, so the skill is in using them together.

Glossary: every word in one line

ADX. Strength of a trend, either way.
Benchmark. An index used to compare against, like the Nifty 50.
Bond. A loan to a government or company that pays interest.
Bollinger Bands. A band showing a price's usual range.
Call. An option giving the right to buy.
Candlestick. One bar showing open, high, low and close.
CMT. Chartered Market Technician, a chart-analysis qualification.
Diversification. Spreading money to reduce risk.
Dividend. A share of a company's profit paid to shareholders.
ETF. A fund bought like a share that holds a basket of assets.
Exchange. A regulated market where shares trade (NSE, BSE).
Expiry. The date a future or option ends.
Fibonacci. Levels (38.2%, 50%, 61.8%) where prices often pause.
Future. An agreement to buy or sell at a fixed price on a fixed date.
Index. A scoreboard of a basket of shares.
Leverage. Controlling a big position with a small deposit.
Long / Short. Profit from a rise / profit from a fall.
Lot. The fixed quantity in one contract.
MACD. A momentum gauge from two moving averages.
Margin. A deposit that backs a futures position.
Moving average. The average price over the last N days.
OBV. A running total of volume on up days minus down days.
Option. The right, not the obligation, to buy or sell.
Portfolio. Everything you hold, plus cash.
Premium. The price paid for an option.
Put. An option giving the right to sell.
RSI. A 0 to 100 speed gauge of recent moves.
Strike price. The fixed price in an option contract.
Support / Resistance. A price floor / a price ceiling.
Trend. The general direction of a price.
Volatility. How much a price swings.
Volume. How many shares were traded.
Z-score. How far a price is from its 20-day average, in standard deviations.
